# Curtain Call — Narrative Brief

**Status:** Exploratory narrative direction, not a locked script.  
**Audience:** Development team / agents. Contains ending spoilers.

## Player-facing hook

**You stole the Lantern to save someone you love.**

Traverse shifting realms of memory, steal the shadows of your pursuers, and fight through guardians who protect what has been. Grow stronger with each journey, uncover fragments of the truth, and draw closer to the final door.

*A roguelite of shadows and memory.*

## Core dramatic premise

Someone the protagonist loves faces death. The protagonist steals a Lantern that can seize an opponent's shadow—their impending action—and wield it themselves. They believe this power can be used to confront Death and save their loved one.

Death is a character, but likely never visibly appears. Death **allowed** the Lantern to be stolen, understanding that this protagonist may need to make the journey rather than be told its lesson. That permission is consequential: the Lantern genuinely disrupts the relationship between actions and their outcomes. Death has not created a harmless test.

The story is not a simple argument that fate overrides free will. People retain meaningful agency even within the constraints of what has happened. The past shapes what can happen next, but does not dictate every choice.

## Two layers of the world

- **Standees — what is.** The physical game board, where encounters and movement occur.
- **Shadow puppets — what could be.** Visible prospective attacks or intentions, which can be stolen before they occur.

The protagonist has little or no direct combat capability without a stolen shadow. Taking a guardian's shadow both interrupts its attack and grants the protagonist an action to use. The guardian's standee remains on the board, but its shadow puppet disappears while stolen.

The protagonist's standee and shadow look nearly identical, unlike the guardians' initially exaggerated, threatening shadows. This reflects the protagonist's singular fixation and is an important visual motif.

## The guardians

The pursuers are custodians of the memories of the dead, and thus protect the integrity of what has happened and its consequences. They are genuine obstacles, not secretly harmless actors. Their shadows can be stolen, temporarily compromising their capacity to protect the memories entrusted to them. Routine combat need not destroy those memories.

Their appearance changes as the journey progresses. Initially their standees and shadows differ wildly, with aggressive or monstrous shadows. Over time their representations shift or exchange qualities: standees become stranger while shadows become more recognizable, vulnerable, or human. The protagonist is learning to perceive preservation rather than merely obstruction. Avoid explaining this explicitly to the player.

## The journey: from fantasy toward reality

The protagonist believes they are crossing realms of the dead to confront Death. The landscapes increasingly resemble memories rather than a literal underworld. Across repeated runs, the world changes and familiar places or objects surface.

1. **Realm of Shadows.** Grand fantasy, clear heroic quest, threatening guardians and exaggerated shadow puppets.
2. **The Unraveling.** Familiar details intrude; the guardians' standee/shadow relationship shifts; the nature of the journey becomes uncertain.
3. **The World That Is.** Fantasy gradually gives way to recognizable reality. The final door leads to the truth the protagonist has avoided.

The theatrical paper-cut language should persist even as the environments become ordinary.

## Roguelite structure

- Each run is another traversal through the shifting landscape of memory.
- The protagonist retains **parts** of prior attempts, represented through persistent unlocks rather than perfect recollection.
- An unlock tree may mix powers, new encounters, environmental changes, remembered fragments, and conditional narrative discoveries. Some nodes require particular actions or prior discoveries rather than currency.
- The player can reach a **locked final door** before being ready to open it, akin to an initially inaccessible true ending. Mechanical victories and incremental unlocks remain meaningful.
- Runs should primarily be enjoyable tactical adventures: stealing shadows, evading attacks, and combining abilities. The game must **not** demand a confrontation with grief every run.
- Narrative revelations should be infrequent and cumulative, not repeated emotional monologues.

## Ending — internal spoiler

The ultimate confrontation with Death is **not** a boss fight. Death may never appear at all. The protagonist enters an ordinary room and encounters the person they sought to save, or the truth of their loss. Their own shadow, for the first time, may show them grieving rather than mirroring their determined pose. They put down the Lantern.

The climax is choosing to face what is, rather than stealing one more possibility. Do not overexplain whether the loved one is still dying or already dead, what Death knew, or the precise mechanics of the final room. The emotional resolution matters more than cosmological certainty.

## Thematic guardrails

- **Saving, not stopping:** player-facing motivation is love and an attempt to save someone, not a pre-announced lesson about accepting loss.
- **Protect the reveal:** no bedside scene, grief tableau, or explicit act of putting down the Lantern in promotional materials.
- **Death is not a villain or therapist:** allowing the theft is risky and consequential; Death cannot make a human's choices for them.
- **Guardians have a real duty:** avoid reducing them to malicious monsters or benign props.
- **Mystery over exposition:** consistent underlying rules, selectively revealed.
- **Avoid moralizing:** the protagonist's attempt to save someone is understandable; the eventual acceptance should not retroactively make their love or struggle foolish.

## Open design questions (deliberately unresolved)

- What precisely happens to a guardian and its protected memories when a shadow is stolen for an extended time?
- What kinds of shadow abilities and combinations make repeated tactical play compelling?
- Which unlocks are mechanical, which are recognitions, and what conditions open the final door?
- How do world transformations distribute across acts within a run versus permanent progression between runs?
- What is the smallest amount of narrative revelation needed for the ending to feel earned?
