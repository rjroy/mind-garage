# Borrowed Turns morning brief

**Try it before reading spoilers:** [One board, one turn, no launch required](borrowed-turns-blind-position.md). It's a reachable position in the shipped game, not a proposed new ability. Skip it if you don't feel like playing. The three reports below contain solutions.

## Key findings

**The first capture is an entry fee in the three audited fights.** You start with no way to attack, and friendly fire cannot finish the last surviving enemy. But with two enemies, the first weapon can do more than hit its owner back. From the same Switcher + B position, keeping the source alive won on turn 8 at 7 HP; two verified routes that killed it or replaced its weapon also won on turn 8, at 6 HP. The difference is what you could make happen along the way, not a persistent reward for having one more HP. Whether making that choice feels good to Ronald is still unknown.

- **[Tactical choice audit](borrowed-turns-tactical-choice-audit.md):** In Anchor, delaying acquisition can simply cost health or an extra turn for the same finish. In Circler, *where* you take the attack changes the next board. In Switcher + B, a player can keep the source of Swap alive and turn the enemies' announced attacks against each other. From the same reachable fork, keeping, cashing out or replacing the source all admit wins. That is evidence of different plans under the existing rules, not proof that any plan is fun or globally optimal.
- **[Puzzle probe and answer key](borrowed-turns-puzzle-insight-probe.md):** A reachable board rewards understanding a particular interaction. An ordinary dodge is enough for safety; using the interaction can also damage an enemy. So there is a real inference with a payoff, but victory or survival does not force its discovery. A changed-board transfer was tested in the model; that second board was not shown reachable in normal play. No player was tested.
- **[Timing comparison](borrowed-turns-timing-comparison.md):** On the same Circler opening, each clock allows the same dodge, capture or reposition. Under the current rule, arming early is as effective as arming at the last instant. A countdown adds a deadline for reading and confirming, not a timed interception. With pause, you can finish your plan and then have to wait for the enemy. A short parry window or continuously moving enemies could offer a genuinely different game, but those change the rules; none of the real-time modes was built or playtested.

## My recommendation

**Try one interesting decision before building one more feature.** Use the blind position above. First ask, “What do I want to make happen here?” Then, after seeing the result, “Do I want another *different position* like this?” That second question is more informative for a puzzle direction than asking you to replay a board you just solved. You needn't log an answer. If you do, distinguish finding a line interesting, enjoying executing it, and choosing to play another.

If the position makes you want another, pursue short two-source encounters that bring a consequential choice forward rather than making you play through setup turns. If you like finding the plan but not clicking through it, test quicker **untimed** execution. If what sounds appealing is catching an attack at the last possible instant, test a paper timing window as a **different core mechanic**. If none of those appeals even on this selected example, don't spend another iteration polishing the catalog. The bots established possibilities, not a reason for you to care about them.

The [online evidence and limits](borrowed-turns-tactics-puzzles-and-time.md) explain why these are separate hypotheses. The [CHI PLAY pacing study](https://doi.org/10.1145/3410404.3414248) documents competing player preferences, not a winning timing format; the [*Portal* study](https://doi.org/10.1145/3116595.3116628) explains why a model solution can't establish what a player inferred.
